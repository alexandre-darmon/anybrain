# Why discovery interviews fail (fake sample article)

Most product teams run discovery interviews, and most of those interviews produce nothing the team can act on. The problem is rarely the people interviewed. It is the questions.

When you ask someone whether they would use a feature, you are asking them to predict their own behaviour in a situation they have not lived. People are generous, optimistic and eager to help, so they say yes. The team hears enthusiasm and builds. A few months later, nobody uses the feature, and the interviews look like a waste of time.

The fix is to ask about the past instead of the future. Instead of "would you use a tool that does this?", ask "tell me about the last time you had this problem". Instead of "how much would you pay?", ask "what did you do about it, and what did that cost you?". Past behaviour is a fact. It can be checked, compared across people and turned into a decision. Future intent is an opinion, and opinions about one's own future are the least reliable kind.

A second habit matters as much as the questions: cadence. Teams that interview once a quarter treat each round as a project, with a script, a recruiting effort and a report that arrives after the decisions were made. Teams that talk to one customer every week keep the questions small and the learning continuous. Each conversation answers one current question, and the answer reaches the team while it still matters.

Interviews test whether a problem is worth solving. They do not test whether a design is usable; that needs a prototype and someone trying to use it. Mixing the two in one conversation is a common reason why interviews feel inconclusive: the team asks about value and gets feedback about buttons.

Three rules summarise the approach. Ask about the last time, not the next time. Talk to someone every week. Keep value questions and usability tests apart. None of this needs a research team, only the discipline to stop asking people to guess.
